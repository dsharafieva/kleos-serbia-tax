Three taxes. Here’s what each one costs

Serbia taxes an entrepreneur’s profit at 10%, a company’s at 15% and again on payout, and a self-assessing freelancer at 10% or 20% depending on which deduction they pick. Contributions are where the four routes actually diverge.

An entrepreneur pays 10% on profit. A company pays 15%, then 15% again on payout. A freelancer picks each quarter: 20% after a fixed deduction, or 10% after a smaller one plus 34% of gross.

Watch continuously

The $59,000 line

Cross about $59,000 of turnover in a calendar year and the flat-rate regime closes. Invoice date decides the year, not payment date — a December invoice settled in January counts as December.

Before you sign
Deadlines are the contractor’s problem. The contract is yours. Serbia judges independence client by client, and six of the nine criteria it uses are written by you, not filed by them. All nine are further down this page.

Nine criteria decide
if this is employment

Serbia has a written misclassification test: nine criteria, assessed separately for each client. Tick five and your contractor is no longer independent of you, and everything they invoiced you gets taxed a different way.

You set their working hours, or their time off needs your approval without a matching cut in fee

They normally work from premises or a place you designate

You run or organise their training or professional development

You found them through a job advertisement or a recruitment agency

You supply or fund their tools, or you manage how the work gets done beyond briefing and checking results

You are 70% or more of their income in a 12-month period

Your contract doesn’t put the usual business risk on them

Your contract bars other clients, beyond a limited number of your direct competitors

They have worked for you 130 or more days in a 12-month period, counting any part of a day

Five or more and the income is re-taxed as other income: 20% of the gross with no deduction for costs, plus 24% pension, 10.3% health and interest. It runs backwards across the whole engagement, to the limitation period — not just the year under audit.

Which of the four models they’re on, whether a d.o.o. is coming, and whether they filed by 15 December. All three change the number they quote you.

Which model they’re on

Flat-rate, bookkeeping, freelancer and company don’t converge — the effective burden on the same invoice can run from roughly 12% to nearly 40%. You’re not choosing it, but you are pricing against it, and only the two entrepreneur routes carry independence-test exposure.

ic4

Why contractors move to a d.o.o.

A one-person company is the recognised way out of the independence test, which is why long-running IT contractors migrate to one. It costs more — 15% on profit, then 15% again on payout — and it doesn’t settle substance-over-form or labour-law questions. It does take the nine criteria off the table.

ic5

The 15 December deadline

The personal-salary election is filed on form OLZ by 15 December for the following year. If your contractor missed it, the personal-salary route is unavailable for twelve months — which tends to surface as a rate request in January.

The questions

The eight that come up most when companies hire in Serbia.

Our contractor already has a registered company in Serbia. What does a platform add?

Registration is their side of the arrangement, and it is genuinely done. The independence test is the other side, and most of it is yours — hours, premises, training, equipment, business risk, exclusivity, and how you found them. A properly registered entrepreneur on a contract that scores five of nine is still a reclassification case. The registration doesn’t fix the engagement.

Who actually pays the tax — us or the contractor?

The contractor. They file and pay their own tax and contributions under whichever of the four models they are on. Your job is paying the agreed invoice — and the rate you agree decides what is left of it after their taxes.

What happens if the independence test is failed?

The income from that client is re-taxed as other income: 20% on gross with no deduction for costs, plus 24% pension and disability and 10.3% health if they are not insured elsewhere. It reaches back across the engagement, not just the audited year.

Is contracting in Serbia a bad idea, then?

No, and we would rather say so plainly. Contracting is the normal, cheaper and usually correct way to work with people in Serbia, and most local advisers will tell you the same. It has one specific, scoreable failure mode. That is a reason to draft the contract carefully, not a reason to employ everyone. Kleos acts as Contractor of Record, so the contract, the classification check and the payments sit with us rather than with your team.

Flat-rate, bookkeeping, freelancer or company — which should they be on?

Not your call, but it moves your costs. Flat-rate is a fixed monthly bill and the simplest, capped at about $59,000 a year. Bookkeeping with the personal-salary election is the only route where the effective rate falls as income rises. The self-assessing freelancer route needs no registration at all and gets more expensive in a straight line. A company suits reinvestment, pays twice on anything distributed, and is the only one of the four outside the independence test.

Do we need to worry about the $59,000 limit?

Only indirectly, and it is worth tracking. Above about $59,000 of turnover in a calendar year the flat-rate regime closes and your contractor moves to full bookkeeping. Invoice date decides which year it falls in, not payment date — a December invoice settled in January counts as December.

Can we hire employees in Serbia, or use an Employer of Record (EOR)?

Not directly — the employer has to be a Serbian entity or a locally registered Employer of Record. Kleos can be the second: we become the legal employer, so the Serbian contract, the dinar payroll and the contributions sit with us. Budget 15.15% in employer contributions on top of gross. The floor is about $3.65 net per hour, and there is no at-will employment — read the termination rules first.

How current are these numbers, and why dollars?

They reflect 2026 rules — the roughly $337 monthly allowance, 24% and 10.3% contributions, 20% VAT above about $79,000, 15% corporate tax. Several are indexed annually, so check them in January. Dollars because the rates are percentages and don’t move with the exchange rate; the thresholds are dinars, and those bind.
